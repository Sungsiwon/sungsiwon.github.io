A **manufacturing data space** is a shared ecosystem in which companies exchange manufacturing and product data through common standards **while each keeps control of its own data**. It is not a central platform that collects everyone's data in one place. The data stays with its owner, and the owner decides who may use it, when, and how. People call this data sovereignty, and it is the idea everything else rests on.

Four names come up again and again, and they are easy to mix up:

- **Gaia-X:** a European framework of shared principles for trusted, sovereign data sharing, run by an association.
- **Catena-X:** a data ecosystem for the automotive value chain.
- **Manufacturing-X:** a German initiative of business, government, and academia that extends the idea to manufacturing as a whole, building on Gaia-X.
- **Korean Manufacturing-X:** a program of Korea's Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources to develop and pilot a standard model for industrial data spaces from 2026 to 2029.

## Why data spaces are on the agenda now

Inside a single factory, the factory's own data is often enough for AI. A growing set of problems, though, can only be solved when data flows between companies. Examples include carbon reporting, part traceability, and responding to supply chain disruptions. Germany's Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs and Energy describes the need for a shared, open data ecosystem in which everyone in the value chain can expect security, sovereignty, and interoperability when sharing product and manufacturing data.

Regulation is moving in the same direction. The EU Data Act has applied since 12 September 2025. According to the European Commission, it gives business users in industries such as manufacturing access to data about the performance of their industrial equipment.

## What "sovereignty" means in practice

Plattform Industrie 4.0's Manufacturing-X FAQ defines data sovereignty as companies deciding for themselves who may access their data, when, and how. It also explains the name: the "X" stands for exchange, and Manufacturing-X builds on Gaia-X. In other words, the shared layer is the rules and the connectors. The data itself is not shared by default.

## What it is used for

The use cases both German sources highlight are supply-chain problems that no single company can answer alone:

- traceability of materials and products
- product carbon footprint and ESG reporting
- the digital product passport
- circular economy
- capacity management
- complex quality assurance and collaborative engineering

The ministry reports that, as of October 2025, 124 companies and research institutions were involved and 12 industries were connected through Manufacturing-X. It also states an explicit goal: companies of every size, especially SMEs and startups, should be able to join easily and share the benefits.

## What Korea is doing

Korea's Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources has set out a timeline:

- **3 September 2025:** at the first Industrial AI Expo, associations from ten major industries signed an MOU on voluntary sharing of manufacturing data between companies.
- **23 October 2025:** the ministry co-hosted the second Korea–Germany Industrial Data Cooperation Forum with Germany's economy ministry. It noted that the 2026 budget proposal included building a standard model for industrial data spaces, the "Korean Manufacturing-X" platform. The program is planned at about KRW 30 billion over 2026–2029. At the same forum, the German side presented progress on Catena-X.
- **10 April 2026:** the ministry issued a call for new R&D projects to develop and pilot the Korean Manufacturing-X platform standard model.

## A practical first step for a manufacturer

You do not need to wait for the standard model to be finished. Make a list of the data you already exchange with customers and suppliers by spreadsheet or email: carbon figures, lot histories, inspection results. Then check whether the format, meaning the field names and units, differs from one partner to the next. That list is where any data space connection will start, and the format differences are exactly what common standards are meant to remove.
